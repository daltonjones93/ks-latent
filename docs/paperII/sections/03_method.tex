%======================================================================
\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%======================================================================

This section restates the model of Paper~I in the form the filter needs,
proves that its observation operator is exactly local, and defines the
filter, the shifted tilings and the physics-based comparator.
Figure~\ref{fig:concept} summarizes the construction; the settings of the
model and the filter are in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The local latent field}
\label{sec:local_field}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\para{Lattice and latent.} A scalar field $u$ on a periodic domain
$\Omega$ is sampled on a uniform grid of spacing $\Delta x$ and observed
at snapshot interval $\Delta t$; it is standardized with one mean and one
standard deviation computed on the training data. The domain is tiled by
non-overlapping square \emph{patches} of $p$ grid points per side, of
physical size $h=p\,\Delta x$, whose centres form a periodic \emph{site
lattice} $\Lat$ with $P=|\Lat|$ sites. We write $\dist(j,k)$ for the
periodic distance between sites in units of the site spacing and $\Tr_s$
for the translation of the physical field by $s$ grid points. The latent
state is a field on the lattice,
\begin{equation}
  z = (z_j)_{j\in\Lat}, \qquad z_j \in \R^{c},
  \qquad \dlat = cP,
  \label{eq:latent_field}
\end{equation}
with $c$ channels per site, flattened site-major only to call a library
routine.

\para{Encoder and decoder.} The encoder $\E$ maps each patch to a
hidden vector by a strided convolution whose kernel equals the patch, and
then to $c-1$ learned channels by per-site layers; channel $0$ is not
learned but is the patch average of $u$, which fixes one coordinate to a
physical quantity with the same meaning at every size. The decoder $\Dec$
mirrors it: per-site layers from $c$ channels to a hidden width, and a
transposed convolution with kernel and stride equal to the patch, which
writes each site's hidden vector back onto its own patch. In general
$\E$ and $\Dec$ may also mix neighbouring sites, over $\Renc$ and $\Rdec$
sites; the model used here has $\Renc=\Rdec=0$. It is \emph{per-patch}:
$z_j$ is a function of patch $j$ alone, and patch $j$ is reconstructed
from $z_j$ alone. This is the property the filter rests on.

\para{Propagator.} The latent dynamics are a residual map
$z^{n+1}=\Fp(z^n)=z^n+f_\theta(z^n)$, where $f_\theta$ embeds each site's
$c$ channels to a width $d_m$, applies $n_\ell$ blocks that each mix the
sites within radius $w$ by an unconstrained circular convolution and then
apply a per-site MLP, both with layer normalization and residual
connections, and projects back to $c$ channels. One step at site $j$
therefore depends on the sites within the reach $\Rprop=w\,n_\ell$, here
$6$ sites. All operators are weight-shared and of finite reach on a
periodic lattice, so the three maps commute exactly with shifts by whole
patches and their parameters do not depend on $P$ (Paper~I,
Prop.~1). The same trained weights define the model on every periodic
domain whose sides are multiples of $h$, with $\dlat$ growing in
proportion to the area; this is how one model is deployed below on
domains from $44^2$ to $440^2$.

\para{Training on windows.} Paper~I trains either on a small periodic box or on
random windows of one large periodic simulation; the models of this paper use
windows, the route that also serves Kolmogorov flow (Paper~I, Sec.~VI\,C). For the 2D KS model
of this paper the windows are $40\times40$ sites ($220^2$) of one
$264^2$ simulation (Sec.~\ref{sec:ks2d}); the assimilation experiments
run up to $440^2$, larger than the training simulation itself. The autoencoder is
trained first, on reconstruction with two regularizers on the channel
covariance (a variance anchor and a log-determinant barrier against
correlated collapse). With the encoder frozen, the propagator is then
trained on rollouts in which a halo of $\eta\ge\Rprop$ sites at the window
edges is overwritten after every step by the true encoding, and only the
free interior is scored; this trains exactly the limited-area problem with
true lateral boundary values (Paper~I, Prop.~2). The rollout length is
ramped to $32$ steps, and Gaussian noise added to the initial latent state
is annealed from $0.10$ to $0.02$.

\para{Trained only on noisy data.} The model used in this paper never sees
a clean field. Every training field carries one fixed realization of
independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation $0.1$ in standardized
units, the level of the observation errors of Sec.~\ref{sec:da_results}.
The reconstruction loss is measured against these noisy fields, and the
propagator learns from encodings of them, so the training data are what an
observing system could provide on a dense network. Section~\ref{sec:da_noisy}
compares this model with the same recipe trained on clean fields.

\subsection{Latent ensemble data assimilation}
\label{sec:da_method}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Observations are taken of the physical field,
$y_n = \Hobs(u_n) + \nu_n$, $\nu_n\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)$, where $\Hobs$
selects grid points on a regular network. The filter state is an
ensemble $\{z^{(e)}\}_{e=1}^{\Nens}$ of latent fields, forecast with
$\Fp$ plus additive model noise, and the observation operator in latent
space is $H = \Hobs\circ\Dec$. Nothing in the filter uses the equations
of the system: the model is the learned propagator, and the only
physical input is the observation network.

\para{The per-site filter.} The 2D experiments use an ensemble Kalman
filter in the local-analysis form of \citet{hunt2007}, run once per site of
the lattice. Site $s$ is updated from the observations whose taper value
$\rho_i$ with respect to $s$ is positive, each with its error variance
divided by $\rho_i$ (R-localization), using the perturbed-observation
update \citep{burgers1998,houtekamermitchell1998}. The taper is the
Gaspari--Cohn function \citep{gaspari1999} of the toroidal site distance
$\dist(j,k)$, with half-width $1.5$ sites. After each analysis the
ensemble spread is relaxed towards the forecast spread, coordinate by
coordinate, with weight $\alpha_{\mathrm R}=0.7$ (relaxation to prior spread, RTPS;
\citealp{whitakerhamill2012}); the forecast adds Gaussian model noise of
standard deviation $0.02$ per latent coordinate at every propagator step.
The 1D experiments of Sec.~\ref{sec:da_1d} also use a particle flow filter
\citep{huvanleeuwen2021} and a stochastic ensemble Kalman filter with
Schur-product localization; all analysis schemes are written out in
Appendix~\ref{app:da_filters}.

The per-patch decoder makes the observation operator exactly local in
latent space.

\begin{proposition}[Block-diagonal observation Jacobian]
\label{prop:blockdiag}
Let $\Rdec=0$ and let $\Hobs$ select grid points. Then
$\partial H_i/\partial z_{j,k} = 0$ for every channel $k$ unless
observation $i$ lies in patch $j$. Consequently:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] the full Jacobian $J\in\R^{m\times\dlat}$ is recovered exactly
  from $c$ forward-mode Jacobian--vector products with the tangents
  $v^{(k)} = \sum_{j\in\Lat} e_{j,k}$, $k=1,\dots,c$, for any $P$ and any
  number of observations $m$;
\item[(b)] in the per-site filter with R-localization, with either the
  perturbed-observation or the ensemble-transform update, the analysis at
  site $s$ depends only on the ensemble at $s$ and at the sites that own an
  observation within the taper support of $s$, and no row of $J$ is
  truncated in computing it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}. The proposition is elementary,
an immediate consequence of the per-patch decoder: it restores in a learned
latent space the property that a physical-space LETKF observing grid points
has by construction, namely that each observation depends on one location.
Its use lies in what it enables. The per-site analysis needs no truncation
of $H$, so the only approximations of the filter are the usual ones of a
localized, finite ensemble with a gain linearized at the ensemble mean
(Appendix~\ref{app:da_filters}); \emph{exact} in this paper refers to the
locality of the observation operator, not to the filter. The same holds on
every domain size and on every shifted tiling (Sec.~\ref{sec:tilings_method}).
It holds for observations of grid-point values, or of averages within one
patch, and not for spatially integrated observations, such as radiances,
whose operator reaches several patches. Part (a) is a column colouring
of $J$ in the sense of \citet{curtis1974}: all columns belonging to the
same channel can share one colour, because no row has two non-zeros in
such a group. With $\Rdec>0$, row $i$ would have non-zeros at every site
within $\Rdec$ of its patch, the colouring would need
$c(2\Rdec+1)^{\dim}$ colours, and the per-site analysis in (b) would have
to include those sites as well or truncate $H$. Neither the cost of the
colouring nor the memory of the per-site filter depends on the domain
size. Figure~\ref{fig:concept}b shows the measured Jacobian.

\subsection{What the nonlinear decoder costs the filter}
\label{sec:nonlinear}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Analysing in latent coordinates changes the statistical problem the
filter solves, and not only its size. The observation errors are Gaussian
in physical space, but the filter's state is $z$, and the map between the
two is nonlinear.

\para{The exact problem and the filter's approximation.} With the
forecast ensemble as prior, the latent analysis targets
\begin{equation}
  p(z\mid y) \;\propto\; \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\big\|y-\Hobs(\Dec(z))\big\|^2_{R^{-1}}\Big)\,p(z).
  \label{eq:latent_posterior}
\end{equation}
The likelihood is Gaussian in $y$ but, because $H=\Hobs\circ\Dec$ is
nonlinear, not in $z$. The per-site filter replaces $p(z)$ by a Gaussian
with the ensemble's mean and covariance and builds its gain from the
Jacobian $J$ at the ensemble mean; its innovation uses the nonlinear $H$
(Appendix~\ref{app:da_filters}). This is one Gauss--Newton step on
Eq.~\eqref{eq:latent_posterior}, exact for a linear $H$ and a Gaussian
prior up to sampling. A physical-space LETKF that observes grid points
has an exactly linear operator and does not make this step; the latent
filter does.

\para{A nonlinearity index.} Write a member as $z^{(e)}=\bar z+\delta^{(e)}$ and
\begin{equation}
  H(\bar z+\delta) = H(\bar z) + J\delta + r(\delta),\qquad
  r(\delta) = \tfrac12\,\delta^\top\nabla^2 H\,\delta + O(|\delta|^3).
  \label{eq:nl_expansion}
\end{equation}
The filter treats $r$ as zero. We measure it on the forecast ensemble by
\begin{equation}
  \eta = \frac{\operatorname{rms}_{e,i}\, r_i(\delta^{(e)})}{\operatorname{rms}_{e,i}\,(J\delta^{(e)})_i},
  \qquad
  \eta_o = \frac{\operatorname{rms}_{e,i}\, r_i(\delta^{(e)})}{\sigma_o},
  \label{eq:nl_index}
\end{equation}
the size of the neglected term relative to the linear one and to the
observation error $\sigma_o$. To the filter, $r$ acts as an additional
observation error that depends on the state and is correlated with it.
Were it independent of the state, it would add a variance of about
$\eta_o^2\sigma_o^2$ to $R$, a relative change of order $\eta_o^2$; because it is
correlated with the state and has a non-zero mean, its effect on the
analysis mean can be of first order in $\eta_o$. An index $\eta_o\ll1$ is
therefore necessary for the linearization to be adequate, and we measure
its cost directly by relinearizing the update
(Appendix~\ref{app:da_filters}).
Because $r$ is second order in $\delta$, $\eta$ grows in proportion to the
prior spread, so the index is small when the cycle is short and the
observations are dense enough to keep the spread small against the
curvature scale of the decoder, and large otherwise. Section~\ref{sec:da_nonlinear}
measures $\eta_o$ and the cost of the linearization directly, by
relinearizing the update (Appendix~\ref{app:da_filters}).

\begin{remark}[The curvature is local too]
\label{rem:local_curvature}
Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:blockdiag}, $\partial^2 H_i/\partial z_{j,k}\,\partial z_{l,k'}=0$
unless $j=l=s(i)$. The residual $r_i$ therefore depends only on the
perturbation at the site that owns observation $i$; the nonlinearity
never couples sites, $\eta$ is a per-site quantity measured in the same
way at every domain size, and a relinearized update needs the same $c$
forward passes per iterate as the first (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).
\end{remark}

\para{Gaussian in which coordinates.} A Kalman update assumes a Gaussian
prior in the coordinates in which it is applied. If forecast errors were
Gaussian in $u$, their image under $\E$ would not be Gaussian in $z$, and
a Gaussian ensemble in $z$ decodes to a non-Gaussian one in $u$; with a
nonlinear decoder, "Gaussian in $z$" and "Gaussian in $u$" are different
assumptions. Neither is exact for KS, whose forecast errors are shaped by
the nonlinear dynamics in either space. The latent coordinates play the
role of a Gaussian anamorphosis \citep{bertino2003,simonbertino2009}, a
change of variables before a Gaussian update, but one learned for
reconstruction rather than for Gaussianity; it may bring the prior closer
to Gaussian or move it away. Which happens is an empirical question,
tested in Appendix~\ref{app:da_gauss}.

\para{Model noise.} The forecast adds isotropic Gaussian noise $\xi$ of
standard deviation $q$ to every latent coordinate. In physical space this
is $\Dec(z+\xi)-\Dec(z) = J_{\Dec}\xi + \tfrac12\xi^\top\nabla^2\Dec\,\xi + \dots$,
with covariance $q^2 J_{\Dec}J_{\Dec}^\top + O(q^4)$: noise that depends on
the state, is confined to each patch ($J_{\Dec}$ is block-diagonal), and
is non-Gaussian at second order. Isotropic in latent coordinates is not
isotropic in physical ones.

\para{Which mean.} Under squared error the best estimate of the field is
its posterior mean, $\Exp[\Dec(z)\mid y]$, approximated by the mean of the
decoded members, which differs from the decoded mean $\Dec(\bar z)$ by
$\tfrac12\tr(\nabla^2\Dec\,B_a)+\dots$, again per patch. The analyses
reported in this paper are means of the blended decoded members; the
difference from blending the decoded means is
at most $0.001$ at $44^2$ on each of three seeds (Sec.~\ref{sec:da_nonlinear}).

\subsection{Shifted tilings}
\label{sec:tilings_method}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A per-patch decoder reconstructs each patch from its own site, so its
errors are tied to the patch lattice: they are largest at patch edges and
they do not average out over time, because the lattice does not move. We
remove this lattice dependence without retraining. Let $\Tr_o$ translate
the physical field by $o\in\{0,p/2\}^2$ grid points, giving $K=4$ copies
of the patch lattice offset by half a patch in $x$, in $y$ and in both.
Tiling $k$ encodes $\Tr_{o_k}u$, runs its own ensemble and its own
per-site filter with the same observations, and decodes its analysis mean
back to the unshifted frame, $\bar u_k = \Tr_{o_k}^{-1}\Dec(\bar z_k)$. The
analysis is the blend
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \bar u = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \omega_k\,\bar u_k,\qquad
  \omega_k(x,y) = \frac{\phi(x-o_{k,x})\,\phi(y-o_{k,y})}
  {\sum_{l}\phi(x-o_{l,x})\,\phi(y-o_{l,y})},\\
  \phi(\xi) = \sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi(\xi \bmod p + \tfrac12)}{p}\Big),
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
a partition of unity of Hann weights whose weight for tiling $k$ is smallest on that
tiling's own patch edges (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}c,d); for $K=4$ the
denominator is $1$. The members are blended the same way, so the blended
ensemble has a spread. The tilings never exchange information: each
cycles its own ensemble, and the blend is formed only for output, since
re-encoding the blended field on each tiling degrades the analysis
(Sec.~\ref{sec:da_tilings}). The construction is cycle spinning
\citep{coifmandonoho1995} on the patch lattice, applied to the analysis.
It keeps the exactness of Proposition~\ref{prop:blockdiag}: each tiling's
filter is exactly local, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend} is a fixed linear map of
their outputs (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}). It costs $K$ filters, which can
run in parallel.

\subsection{How large an ensemble is needed}
\label{sec:nens}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

An ensemble filter needs enough members to span the directions along
which forecast errors grow. Without localization these are the unstable
directions of the whole attractor, so $\Nens$ must grow with $\DKY$ and
therefore with $|\Omega|$. With localization, each local analysis only
needs to span the unstable directions active inside the taper support
$A_{\mathrm{loc}}$ \citep{patil2001,ott2004}, which suggests
\begin{equation}
  \Nens \gtrsim \rho_{\mathrm{KY}}\,|A_{\mathrm{loc}}|,
  \label{eq:nens_local}
\end{equation}
independent of the total domain size. For 1D KS with the taper used in
Sec.~\ref{sec:da_1d} (support radius $6$ sites of length $6.25$),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nens_local} gives $\Nens\gtrsim 0.22\times75\approx17$; the 2D
value is given in Sec.~\ref{sec:da_2d}. Combined with a model whose
parameters do not depend on the domain, this is the reason to expect a
fixed-size localized filter to keep working as the domain grows, while an
unlocalized filter of the same size degrades.

\subsection{A physics-based comparator}
\label{sec:comparator}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

To ask what the learned model costs or gains against the equations,
Sec.~\ref{sec:da_model_error} runs the same kind of filter in the physical
space of a partial differential equation: an ensemble of PDE states,
forecast with a spectral solver, analysed by a local ensemble transform
Kalman filter on $4\times4$ blocks of grid points with R-localization and
RTPS, observing the same points with the same errors. Its forecast model
is deliberately wrong (Appendix~\ref{app:da_comparator}). It is given the
standard remedies for model error, each tuned on one seed: the RTPS weight
and the localization radius; online estimation of global model parameters
by state augmentation \citep{anderson2001,ruiz2013}, with a global ensemble
transform for the parameters and a floor on their spread; and correction
of the mean one-cycle forecast error, with additive noise drawn from the
covariance of the remaining error \citep{dee2005}, both estimated offline
from $r = u(t+T)-M\big(u(t)\big)$ on an independent trajectory, where $M$
is the wrong model and $T$ one assimilation cycle.

\para{An oracle-forecast latent filter.} To separate what the latent
coordinates cost from what the learned model costs,
Sec.~\ref{sec:da_nonlinear} also runs the latent filter with a perfect
forecast: each member is a physical field, forecast with the spectral
solver of the true equations, encoded on each tiling, analysed by the
per-site filter, and decoded. In the \emph{projected} variant the next
member is the decoded analysis $\Dec(z_a)$, so every member returns to the
decoder's range each cycle; in the \emph{incremental} variant it is
$u_f+\Dec(z_a)-\Dec(z_f)$, so only the analysis increment passes through the
latent coordinates. Against the physical-space LETKF with the same perfect
model, the incremental variant isolates the cost (or gain) of analysing in
latent coordinates, and the difference between the two variants the cost
of the decoder's range; against the learned-model filter, the projected
variant isolates the cost of the learned forecast. The incremental
variant adds no model noise; the projected one, whose spread otherwise
decays because every member returns to the decoder's range, adds Gaussian
noise of standard deviation $0.045$ to each latent coordinate per cycle,
the learned filter's $0.02$ per step over five steps.
