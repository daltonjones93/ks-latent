%======================================================================
\section{Learned versus physics-based filters under model error}
\label{sec:da_model_error}
%======================================================================

No forecast model of a real system is correct, and for many systems of
interest, convection-permitting weather models among them, the model
error is large, structural and poorly known. Measurement error, by
contrast, is usually well characterized, which is why the model of this
section is trained on noisy fields. The question is then not whether a
learned filter beats a filter that knows the equations; with the true
equations the physics-based filter reaches $0.076$, four times better than
the latent filter's $0.31$. About half of that gap is the learned forecast:
with the true equations as its forecast, the latent filter reaches
$0.152$--$0.158$, and the rest, $0.07$--$0.08$, is the cost of analysing in
latent coordinates, mostly through the precision of the decoder
(Sec.~\ref{sec:da_nonlinear}). It is which kinds of model error make a model learned from noisy data the
better choice, once the physics-based filter is given every standard
remedy for model error.

\para{Design.} The truth is the 2D KS equation, the system the latent
model was trained on, and its error, $0.312$ averaged over seeds 0--2
($0.302$--$0.324$), is fixed: it was not tuned for this section. The
comparator is the physical-space local ensemble transform filter of
Sec.~\ref{sec:comparator}, with the same observations and 64 members, whose forecast
model is wrong in one of the following ways (Appendix~\ref{app:da_comparator}; a hidden field and a
missing process follow below):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{a wrong coefficient}, $a\nabla^2u$ with $a=1.1$ or $1.25$;
\item \emph{a spurious process}: uniform advection at speed $1$ or $2$, or a
  fixed terrain-like forcing of amplitude $0.2$ or $0.4$;
\item \emph{a coarse grid}, $64^2$ or $32^2$ instead of $128^2$;
\item \emph{a wrong form of the damping}: $-a\nabla^2u - b(-\nabla^2)^{\gamma/2}u -
  (g/2)|\nabla u|^2$ with damping order $\gamma=5$, $6$ or $8$ instead of $4$, whose three
  coefficients are all estimated online, so that no coefficient is left
  wrong but no setting of them reproduces the KS growth-rate curve.
\end{itemize}
Each physics-based filter is given, and tuned on seed 0 over, the RTPS
weight and the localization radius; online estimation of the parameter
it gets wrong (the coefficient, the advection speed, or $a$, $b$ and $g$
jointly); and bias correction with additive model error estimated
offline from one-cycle residuals on an independent trajectory. Online
estimation augments the state with the parameter and keeps its ensemble
spread above a floor ($0.02$, relative for the damping coefficients),
with the state RTPS retuned with the floor on; without the floor the
parameter spread collapsed within 30--40 cycles and the estimates stopped
short of the truth (Appendix~\ref{app:da_comparator}). Its
reported value is its best configuration on seed 0, confirmed on seeds
1--2. The decision rule (a filter wins if its three-seed mean is at least
$10\%$ lower and it is lower on every seed), the first four kinds of error
and the cubic case below were written down before the runs; the wider tuning
grid, the spread floor, the anisotropic missing process and the grouping of
the results by kind of error came after results were in, and each of them
favoured the physics-based filter or left it unchanged. One further pre-registered case was stopped before its latent runs: a
missing cubic saturation, truth $u_t=\mathrm{KS}(u)-\beta u'^3$ with $u'$ the
mean-removed field. At the pre-registered strength its one-cycle error was
$0.055$ ($0.050$ state-dependent), and the physics-based filter without
remedies reached $0.190$ on seed 0, well below the latent filter's error on
the unperturbed system ($0.31$; no latent model was trained for this truth); at no strength we tried did the state-dependent part exceed $0.06$
(the truth stopped being chaotic first), so the process could not test the
other side, and the anisotropic missing process below replaced it.

\para{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:da_model_error} and Fig.~\ref{fig:model_error} give every case. Where the
error lies in a parameter the physics-based filter can estimate, or in a bias it can remove, it wins or
ties at every magnitude we tested (the $\nabla^2$ coefficient up to $25\%$ off, advection up to speed $2$). Online estimation brings the
$\nabla^2$ coefficient back to $1.00$ ($0.999$--$1.003$ on every seed) from $1.1$ and from $1.25$, and the
filter reaches $0.136$ and $0.164$, $2.3$ and $1.9$ times better than the latent filter; it brings the
spurious advection speed back to zero ($|\hat c|<0.01$) and reaches $0.225$ at speed $1$ ($1.39$ times
better), although that model's one-cycle error, $0.78$, is the largest of any case won. Bias correction
removes the fixed forcing ($0.225$ at both amplitudes). The coarse grids, whose truncation error is
neither a parameter nor a bias, lose little ($0.083$ and $0.181$) because that error is small
($0.000$ and $0.036$ state-dependent). Correcting the parameter does not close the whole gap to the true equations ($0.076$): at
advection speed $2$ the estimate converges within 20 cycles, but the error stays at $0.345$, $1.11$ times
the latent filter's: the latent filter is $9.6\%$ lower, just short of the $10\%$ threshold, so the
case is a tie. We have not isolated what keeps the error there (the inflation needed while the
parameter is uncertain is one candidate).

Where the error is in the form of the equations, no parameter can remove it. With the wrong damping
order the three coefficients $a$, $b$ and $g$ are all estimated, and the filter reaches $0.226$, $0.308$
and $0.409$ for $\gamma=5$, $6$ and $8$: the physics-based filter wins at $\gamma=5$ ($1.38$ times), ties at
$\gamma=6$, and loses at $\gamma=8$ ($1.31$ times, where estimation does not help); bias correction did not help
these state-dependent errors (seed 0: $0.297$, $0.361$ and $0.440$, slightly worse than tuning alone).

\para{What decides the comparison.} Define the model error after the remedy: the state-dependent part of the
one-cycle forecast error (the residual left after subtracting its mean, which bias correction removes) of the
physical model with the parameters the filter converged to, in units of the observation error
(Fig.~\ref{fig:model_error}a). For a wrong coefficient or a spurious advection this is zero: estimation
removes the whole error, and the physics-based filter wins at $a=1.1$, $a=1.25$ and advection $1$, and ties
at advection $2$. For the cases in which no parameter can remove the error it is
$0.09$, $0.15$ and $0.20$ in field units ($0.15$, $0.24$ and $0.33$ of the observation error) at damping
orders $5$, $6$ and $8$, and $0.09$, $0.19$ and $0.28$ ($0.15$, $0.30$ and $0.43$) for the missing process;
the hidden field, with the coefficient estimated, leaves $0.10$ and $0.26$ ($0.12$ and $0.26$). Ordered by
this one quantity the outcomes are physics wins up to $0.15$ of the observation error (damping order $5$),
ties between $0.12$ and $0.26$ (the missing process at $\delta=0.1$, damping order $6$, both hidden fields),
and latent wins from $0.30$ upward (the missing process at $\delta=0.2$ and $0.3$, damping order $8$).
The crossover therefore lies between about a quarter and a third of the observation error, and advection
$2$ is the one exception, a tie at an error of zero. This ordering was found after the results were in
(the first version of the axis used the error before the remedy, which orders the parametric cases
wrongly because the remedy removes it), so we read it as a description, not a tested prediction; the
missing process at $\delta=0.2$ and $0.3$ is the only set of cases whose strength was chosen before it
was located, and both are on the latent side of it. It is a result for one system, one observation network, one cycle
length, one ensemble size and one learned model, not a general constant. The latent filter's error,
which sets the other side of the comparison, depends on all of them: it is $0.226$ with observations
twice as many in each direction and $0.769$ with half as many (Sec.~\ref{sec:da_robust}). Nor is it a
comparison of forecast errors alone: the learned model's own total one-cycle error (not only its
state-dependent part), from the encoded true state and including its decoder floor, is $0.19$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:da_accuracy}; dashed line in Fig.~\ref{fig:model_error}a), so the outcome also depends on
how each filter handles its model's error.

\para{A hidden field.} A separate question is a truth that is not Markov in the observed field, which
neither filter's model can represent: an
unobserved field $v$ with its own slow dynamics, $u_t=\mathrm{KS}(u)+\varepsilon v$,
$v_t=-v/\tau+\kappa\nabla^2u$, $\tau=2$, whose memory (4--4.5 time units, eight to nine cycles) makes $v$ a
function of the past of $u$, not of its present: about $20\%$ of its variance is not predictable from
the current field. This case does not test the crossover. The latent propagator maps the current
latent state to the next, so it is Markov by construction and cannot represent the memory either;
it is trained on noisy fields $u$ of this coupled system but never sees $v$. And because $v$ is
driven by $\nabla^2u$, much of its effect on $u$ looks like a change in the $\nabla^2$ coefficient,
which the physics-based filter, given every remedy (Appendix~\ref{app:da_comparator}), can estimate
online (it settles at $1.12$--$1.14$ at $\varepsilon=0.1$). The physics model's
error is therefore largely parametric, while the latent model's is structural. At $\varepsilon=0.1$
the latent filter's error is $0.476$ ($0.465$--$0.483$) and the physics-based filter's best, with
the $\nabla^2$ coefficient estimated online, $0.430$ ($0.423$--$0.440$), $9.8\%$ lower, just short of the threshold, so a tie; at
$\varepsilon=0.2$ they are $0.628$ ($0.620$--$0.641$) and $0.599$ ($0.596$--$0.604$), a tie. Both
filters lose ground against what the true equations achieve with $v$ unobserved ($0.405$ and
$0.493$, the same filter carrying its own $v$); both stay well below interpolation. Memory of this
kind limits a Markov latent model as much as a misspecified physical one, and the remedy on the
learned side, a propagator given past states, is outside this study.

\para{A missing process.} The hidden field has memory the current field does not show; a missing process
that is Markov in $u$ is a cleaner test of errors in the form of the equations. The truth adds an anisotropic gradient nonlinearity,
$u_t=\mathrm{KS}(u)-\tfrac{\delta}{2}(u_x^2-u_y^2)$ with $\delta=0.1$, $0.2$ and $0.3$, and the
physics-based filter's model is the isotropic KS equation. Joint online estimation of $a$, $b$ and $g$
cannot represent a term that tells $x$ from $y$, so this error is structural, like the wrong damping
order, but its size is set by one parameter. The latent model is retrained on noisy fields of each truth
with the same recipe, and the latent filter keeps its single setting; the errors are in that system's field
units. The physics-based filter was tuned over the full grid of Appendix~\ref{app:da_comparator} on seed
0, with every remedy at its best setting, and so was a filter with the true equations, which reaches
$0.087$, $0.087$ and $0.096$. The state-dependent one-cycle errors are $0.094$, $0.194$ and $0.323$, that is
$0.15$, $0.31$ and $0.49$ of the observation error (with the parameters estimated, $0.093$, $0.189$ and
$0.282$: $0.15$, $0.30$ and $0.43$). The outcome is the one the ordering above gives (Table~\ref{tab:da_model_error}, squares in
Fig.~\ref{fig:model_error}a). At $\delta=0.1$ the latent filter's error is $0.310$ ($0.300$--$0.323$) and
the physics-based filter's $0.328$ ($0.323$--$0.334$): lower on every seed, but by $5.5\%$, a tie. At
$\delta=0.2$ they are $0.341$ ($0.337$--$0.345$) and $0.432$ ($0.421$--$0.439$), and at $\delta=0.3$
$0.376$ ($0.372$--$0.383$) and $0.485$ ($0.470$--$0.496$): the latent filter wins by $1.27$ and $1.29$
times. Online estimation of $a$, $b$ and $g$ lowered the physics-based filter's seed-0 error by $2\%$ at
$\delta=0.2$ and $10\%$ at $\delta=0.3$; bias correction did not help at $\delta=0.2$ and lowered it by $4\%$
at $\delta=0.3$. With the true equations the physics-based filter is about four times better than either,
as for the other cases.

\para{Ensemble budget.} The two filters see the same observations but not the same number of model
states: the latent filter runs four tilings of 64 members (256 forecasts), the physics-based filter 64. At an equal count, four tilings of 16, the latent
filter's error is $0.335$ ($0.318$--$0.352$; Sec.~\ref{sec:da_tilings}) instead of $0.312$, and for the
missing process $0.332$, $0.355$ and $0.395$ at $\delta=0.1$, $0.2$ and $0.3$ (seeds 0--2). The missing-process
outcomes are unchanged at equal count (a tie, then latent wins by $1.22$ and $1.23$ times instead of $1.27$
and $1.29$). Against $0.335$ the other outcomes keep their sign, except advection at speed 2, which
becomes a tie ($1.03$); the hidden-field cases were not rerun at equal count.

\para{Robustness.} The physics-based filter works only in a narrow window
of RTPS. With damping order 6 its error is $0.72$, $0.43$, $0.35$, $0.93$ and $1.27$ at
RTPS $0.5$, $0.6$, $0.7$, $0.9$ and $1.0$ ($L_{\mathrm{loc}}=16$), and multiplicative inflation
without RTPS was far worse in every case with a wrong coefficient, forcing
or advection, and $4$--$21\%$ worse on the coarse grids. At its best setting
it is over-dispersed, with spread over error $1.6$--$1.75$ at damping order 6
and up to $6$ with advection. The
latent filter used one setting throughout and was not retuned for any
case. The comparison does not depend on the domain size: at $220^2$ (seed 0), with online estimation
and the parameter spread kept above $0.02$, the physics-based filter reaches $0.165$ with the
$\nabla^2$ coefficient $25\%$ off and $0.231$ with advection at speed 1, against the latent filter's
$0.312$, physics wins by $1.9$ and $1.35$ times (at $44^2$, seed 0, $1.95$ and $1.42$); both estimates
converge to the true values ($1.001$ and $0.0001$).

\para{What this does and does not show.} The comparison is between a
learned model with complete noisy training fields of the true system and
a physical model with a chosen, not calibrated, error; real training data
are sparser, and real model errors are not of our choosing. The latent
filter's error is about four times that of a filter with the correct
equations, so where a trustworthy model exists it should be used. The
result is a division by the kind of error. Errors that a parameter or a
bias can represent are best handled by the physical model with online
estimation or bias correction, at every magnitude we tested; errors in the form
of the equations are not, and above a state-dependent one-cycle error of
about a quarter to a third of the observation error after the remedy the learned model was the
better choice. That is the part of the model error that parameter
estimation and bias correction cannot reach.
