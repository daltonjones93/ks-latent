\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

\para{What the nonlinear coordinates cost.} Analysing in a learned latent
space replaces a linear observation operator by a nonlinear one and
Gaussian assumptions in physical space by Gaussian assumptions in latent
space (Sec.~\ref{sec:nonlinear}). At the configuration of this paper the
first costs little: the term the filter neglects is about $4\%$ of the
observation error, because one cycle leaves a prior spread at the observed
points below the observation error, and relinearizing the update does
not change the error. The per-patch decoder keeps this cost local, so it is
the same on every domain size. It would grow with the spread, for sparser
or noisier observations and longer cycles.
The coordinates have a larger cost through the decoder's precision: with a
perfect forecast, the latent analysis error is about twice that of a
physical-space filter ($0.152$--$0.158$ against $0.076$--$0.086$) and close
to the reconstruction floor. With the learned forecast, which accounts for
about half of the error, this cost is the smaller one; it would become the
main one for a better forecast model, and a more precise decoder, or one
that reads neighbouring sites, is where it would be reduced.

\para{What locality buys.} The latent field makes localization possible in a
learned space, and with a per-patch decoder it makes the observation
operator exactly local (Proposition~\ref{prop:blockdiag}). The ensemble a localized filter needs is
then set by the local problem, about 32 members per tiling at both sizes
tested, while an unlocalized filter needs more members than the attractor
has unstable directions, a number that grows with the domain
(Secs.~\ref{sec:da_2d} and \ref{sec:da_1d}). The price of exact locality is
an error tied to the patch lattice, which the decoder does not remove; the
shifted tilings remove most of it, because the tilings' errors are only
weakly correlated, and they do so without retraining. The per-patch decoder
has a price of its own: it reconstructs each patch without its neighbours,
which is what ties the error to the lattice. A decoder that reads the
neighbouring sites ($\Rdec=1$) would reduce that error at its source, at the
cost of an observation Jacobian that is banded rather than block-diagonal,
$c\,3^{2}$ colours instead of $c$, and per-site analyses that include the
neighbours (Sec.~\ref{sec:da_method}); the locality is kept, the
block-diagonal form is not. In 1D a per-patch model assimilated about twice
as badly as one whose decoder mixes neighbouring sites
(Sec.~\ref{sec:ks1d}). We have not trained the 2D model with such a decoder,
so we cannot say how much of the tilings' gain repairs an error that the
per-patch design itself creates. The same
construction worked in Kolmogorov flow once the tilings were restricted to
the symmetries the model was trained on. The remaining analysis error is
mostly the filter's, not the decoder's: the blended decoder floor is less
than half of it (Sec.~\ref{sec:da_accuracy}); this is so with the learned
forecast, and with a perfect one the decoder's precision becomes the main
limit (Sec.~\ref{sec:da_nonlinear}). Better use of the ensemble
inside each tiling, and models of the propagator's error in place of
isotropic noise, are the natural next steps.

\para{When to prefer a learned model.} A filter built on the correct
equations is about four times more accurate than the latent filter, and one
built on equations with a small or correctable error is still better. The
comparison of Sec.~\ref{sec:da_model_error} locates the boundary in two
steps. First the kind of error: no error a parameter or a bias could
represent was won by the latent filter once online estimation was kept
alive, at any magnitude we tested (five physics wins and one tie).
Then, for errors in the form of the equations, the part of the one-cycle
forecast error that bias correction cannot remove: the latent filter wins
once it exceeds about a quarter to a third of the observation error after the remedy in the configuration we tested
(one observation network, cycle length, ensemble size and learned model).
The missing anisotropic process,
whose strength we could set, places a tie at $0.15$ of the observation
error and wins at $0.31$ and $0.49$, consistent with that boundary. Two
features of the comparison carry over to real systems. The errors that
parameter estimation and bias correction cannot reach are the structural
ones, a wrong form of an operator or a process that is missing, and those
are the errors of the models of convection and cloud processes that
motivate this work. And the learned model needs no tuning for the error of
the model it replaces: one setting served every case, while the
physics-based filter worked only in a narrow window of its relaxation to
prior spread. The model learned from noisy fields was at least as good as
one learned from clean fields ($4$--$5\%$ better, a consistent gain but below
our $10\%$ threshold), so measurement error, the part of the error
budget that is usually known, can be handled in training. A process with
memory that the current field does not reveal limits both filters
(Sec.~\ref{sec:da_model_error}); propagators with access to past states are
the obvious remedy on the learned side.

\para{Which training route.} Paper~I shows that for 2D KS a model trained on a small periodic box of
side $44$ reproduces the Kaplan--Yorke dimension on larger domains as well as one trained on windows
of a large simulation. Reproducing the dimension does not make a model a good filter model. With this
paper's filter (four tilings, RTPS $0.7$, observations every $2.75$), a 6-channel small-box model reaches
$0.599$ at $44^2$ and $0.813$ at $440^2$, no better than interpolating the observations ($0.819$), and a
12-channel small-box model trained on noisy fields, as ours is, reaches $0.408$ at $44^2$ but $1.104$ at
$440^2$, worse than interpolation; a 6-channel window-trained model gives $0.579$ and $0.563$ (one seed
each). The window-trained models hold their error from $44^2$ to $440^2$ and the small-box models do not,
which is why this paper's model is trained on windows. More training data or a longer reach did not close
the small-box models' gap at $44^2$ either ($0.393$ with six times the data, $0.405$ with reach $5$ on a
$66^2$ box, both 12-channel and at their best RTPS). We have not identified what the filter needs that the dimension does not measure.

\para{Limitations.} We state them plainly.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Twin experiments.} The truth is the system the model was trained
  on, simulated, and observed with known Gaussian errors; real observations
  have representativeness errors and biases that we did not model.
\item \emph{Systems.} The main results are for one system, 2D KS, on
  periodic squares; Kolmogorov flow was assimilated at two widths with one
  model and without a physics-based comparator, and 1D KS with a model that
  is not per-patch.
\item \emph{Observations.} The networks are regular and dense, four
  observations per site in the main experiments, of the full field, and
  aligned with the patch lattice: observations every 8 grid points, patches
  of 16 and tiling offsets of 8, so every tiling sees the same observation
  geometry relative to its patches. Irregular networks and networks not
  commensurate with the patches are untested, and so are spatially
  integrated observations, for which Proposition~\ref{prop:blockdiag} does
  not hold. With
  one observation per site the filter is four times better than
  interpolation and stays bounded over 300 cycles, but on longer runs with
  sparser networks the stability of the learned propagator becomes the
  limit; failsafes for that regime are the subject of a follow-up study.
\item \emph{Model errors.} The errors of the physics-based comparator were
  chosen, not calibrated to a real model, and the crossover is located by
  the cases we chose; its position in other systems remains to be measured.
  We did not compare with the third option, a physical model corrected by a
  learned term trained on the same data \citep{pathak2018hybrid}, which
  targets exactly the state-dependent error on which the crossover rests.
\item \emph{Training data.} The learned model was trained on complete,
  noisy fields of the true system, which real data rarely provide; training
  from sparse observations, by alternating assimilation and training
  \citep{brajard2020,bocquet2020}, is untested with a latent field.
\item \emph{Calibration.} The 2D KS ensemble is well calibrated with four
  tilings, but the Kolmogorov ensemble is under-dispersed, and the
  inflation was not retuned for it.
\item \emph{Model noise.} Model error in the latent forecast is represented
  by isotropic Gaussian noise of fixed size, not by the statistics of the
  learned propagator's own error. Isotropic in latent coordinates, it is
  state dependent, confined to each patch and non-Gaussian at second order
  in physical ones (Sec.~\ref{sec:nonlinear}).
\end{itemize}

\para{Outlook.} The target is the assimilation of convection-permitting
simulations of organized convection, such as squall lines, whose cloud
processes are uncertain and whose state is mostly unobserved; that needs
non-periodic, limited-area domains, which the forced halo of the training
(Paper~I) already treats as its operating mode, and statistics of the
learned model's own error in place of fixed isotropic noise. The
ingredients carry over to any system whose chaos is extensive and whose
dynamics are local at the scale of a few patches.
