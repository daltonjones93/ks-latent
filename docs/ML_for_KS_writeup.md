# ML for the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation: a structured walkthrough

This document describes the modeling pipeline we have built for surrogate modeling and (eventually) data assimilation of the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky (KS) equation. It explains every component, gives the equations they implement, and records the design decisions and iteration history so the choices are explicit rather than implicit.

---

## 1. Problem statement

### 1.1 The KS equation

We work with the 1D Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation on a periodic spatial domain $[0, L)$:

$$
\partial_t u + u\,\partial_x u + \partial_{xx} u + \partial_{xxxx} u \;=\; 0,
\qquad u(x,t) \in \mathbb{R},\quad x \in [0,L),
$$

with periodic boundary conditions $u(0, t) = u(L, t)$. For $L \gtrsim 22$, KS is spatiotemporally chaotic with a finite-dimensional inertial manifold (~22D for $L=22$); the long-time dynamics live on that low-dimensional attractor even though the PDE is infinite-dimensional in principle.

Two properties of KS matter for everything below:

1. **Translation symmetry.** If $u(x,t)$ is a solution, so is $u(x-c, t)$ for any constant $c$. Any data-driven model should be approximately translation-equivariant.
2. **Chaos.** Small perturbations grow at a positive Lyapunov exponent $\lambda > 0$. Predictability is bounded: beyond ~$1/\lambda$ time units, no deterministic forecast can beat climatology.

### 1.2 Discretization and data

Space is discretized on $N_x = 128$ uniform grid points. Each KS trajectory is generated by the spectral solver `KS(u0, nt=4000)`, then we discard a transient and subsample in time:

$$
\{u_n\}_{n=0}^{T} \subset \mathbb{R}^{N_x}, \qquad u_n = u(\,\cdot\,, t_n).
$$

The temporal subsampling stride $s$ is a knob. We mostly use $s = 5$ (after early experiments with $s = 15$).

Training data consists of 60 independent trajectories from 60 different random initial conditions; we use 50 for training and 10 for validation. Snapshots are normalized to zero mean and unit standard deviation using statistics computed once on the training set.

### 1.3 The long-term goal

The ultimate target is **nonlinear data assimilation** for KS: given sparse, noisy observations of $u(x,t)$, recover a good estimate of the full state $u$ and predict its future evolution. Classical DA on chaotic systems requires a forecast model. Our two-stage architecture provides one.

## 2. Architecture overview

We build the forecast model in two stages:

$$
u_n \;\xrightarrow{E_\phi}\; z_n \;\xrightarrow{P_\theta}\; z_{n+1} \;\xrightarrow{D_\psi}\; \hat u_{n+1}.
$$

- **Stage 1 — Autoencoder.** A pair $(E_\phi, D_\psi)$ with $E_\phi: \mathbb{R}^{N_x} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ and $D_\psi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{N_x}$, where $d = 24 \ll N_x = 128$. Learned by reconstruction.
- **Stage 2 — Propagator.** A map $P_\theta: \mathbb{R}^{2d} \to \mathbb{R}^d$, $(z_{n-1}, z_n) \mapsto z_{n+1}$, learned on encoded trajectories.

Forecasts at runtime are produced by encoding the current physical state pair, rolling the propagator forward in latent space, and decoding when needed:

$$
z_{n+k+1} = z_{n+k} + P_\theta(z_{n+k-1}, z_{n+k}), \qquad \hat u_{n+k+1} = D_\psi(z_{n+k+1}).
$$

The reason for two stages: a high-dimensional chaotic flow is hard to forecast in physical space (without architectural inductive biases), but in latent space the state is small and DA updates are cheap. The price is that the latent representation has to be **dynamically meaningful**, not just a good compressor — a key issue that we'll return to in §6.

---

## 3. Stage 1: the autoencoder

### 3.1 Architecture

The encoder is built in the spirit of a *Set Transformer* / *Perceiver*: an MLP lifts the input snapshot into a fixed bank of synthetic tokens, transformer attention runs on those tokens, then a pooled output is projected to the latent code.

Schematically, with $u \in \mathbb{R}^{N_x}$:

1. **Embed:** an MLP with hidden width 512 lifts $u$ to a vector $h \in \mathbb{R}^{N_t \cdot D_m}$ which is reshaped into $N_t = 8$ tokens of dimension $D_m = 128$.
2. **Transform:** 3 transformer-encoder layers (multi-head attention + FFN, dim_ff = 256).
3. **Pool + project:** the tokens are pooled (mean over the token axis) and a linear layer projects to $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $d = 24$.

The decoder mirrors this: $z \mapsto$ tokens $\mapsto$ transformer $\mapsto$ MLP head $\to \hat u \in \mathbb{R}^{N_x}$.

Total parameter count: about 2.0M.

**Why this architecture rather than a CNN?** The original motivation was the global, non-local nature of KS dynamics (the $\partial_x$ operator is global on a periodic domain in the spectral sense). The attention block can in principle capture arbitrary global structure. The downside is that this architecture has no built-in translation equivariance — which matters, as we'll see.

### 3.2 Loss functions

The training objective combines three terms. Let $z = E_\phi(u)$ and $\hat u = D_\psi(z)$. For a batch $\{u^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^B$ with corresponding latents $\{z^{(b)}\}$:

**Reconstruction.** Mean squared error in physical space:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} \;=\; \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \frac{1}{N_x}\|\hat u^{(b)} - u^{(b)}\|_2^2.
$$

**Decorrelation.** Penalize off-diagonal entries of the batch latent covariance to push different latent dimensions to encode different features. Let $\bar z = \frac{1}{B}\sum_b z^{(b)}$, $\tilde z^{(b)} = z^{(b)} - \bar z$, and

$$
\Sigma \;=\; \frac{1}{B-1}\sum_b \tilde z^{(b)}\,\bigl(\tilde z^{(b)}\bigr)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
$$

Then

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}} \;=\; \frac{1}{d(d-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\Sigma_{ij}^2.
$$

**Variance.** Push each latent dimension to have unit variance, to keep them all "alive":

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}} \;=\; \frac{1}{d}\sum_i \bigl(\Sigma_{ii} - 1\bigr)^2.
$$

**Total stage-1 (v1–v4) loss:**

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AE}} \;=\; w_{\text{rec}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + w_{\text{dec}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}} + w_{\text{var}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}},
$$

with $w_{\text{rec}} = 1$, $w_{\text{dec}} = w_{\text{var}} = 0.01$.

### 3.3 Translation augmentation (v4)

Versions v1–v3 of the autoencoder showed an enormous train/val gap (train recon $\to 0.0005$, val recon stuck at $0.24$). This was diagnosed not as under-training but as **memorization**: 20 trajectories were not enough for the model to learn the underlying KS translation symmetry on its own, so it memorized snapshots in specific spatial frames.

The fix in v4 was translation augmentation. At every training step, each snapshot $u$ is replaced by

$$
u_{\Delta x} = \mathrm{Roll}(u, \Delta x), \qquad \Delta x \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{0, 1, \ldots, N_x - 1\},
$$

where $\mathrm{Roll}$ denotes cyclic shift. Since $u_{\Delta x}$ is also a valid KS state, this is a label-noise-free data augmentation: the effective dataset size grows by a factor of $N_x = 128$.

The result was decisive: val recon dropped to $\sim 0.075$, train and val landed within $\sim 1.3{\times}$ of each other, and a shift-invariance scan confirmed the encoder had learned approximate translation equivariance.

The training set was also increased from 20 to 50 trajectories, with the natural diversity of independent initial conditions providing broader attractor coverage.

### 3.4 Dynamics-aware extension (v5)

A separate diagnostic (described in §5) revealed that even the v4 latents were not great for **forecasting**: a propagator trained directly in physical space cleanly outperformed persistence, while a propagator in v4 latent space did not. The interpretation: v4 has no incentive to produce *predictable* latents, only *reconstructible* ones.

The v5 fix augments the training objective with an end-to-end physical-space pushforward. Let $(u_{n-1}, u_n, u_{n+1}, u_{n+2})$ be a window of consecutive snapshots, and define

$$
z_n = E_\phi(u_n), \qquad
(\hat z_{n+1}, \hat z_{n+2}) = P_{\text{aux}}\bigl(z_{n-1}, z_n;\, K_{\text{pred}}{=}2\bigr),
\qquad \hat u_{n+k} = D_\psi(\hat z_{n+k}),
$$

where $P_{\text{aux}}$ is a small auxiliary propagator (~22k params, see §4 for the architecture). The prediction loss is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} \;=\; \frac{1}{K_{\text{pred}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\text{pred}}} \frac{1}{N_x}\|\hat u_{n+k} - u_{n+k}\|_2^2.
$$

The v5 total loss is

$$
\boxed{\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{AE,v5}} \;=\; w_{\text{rec}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + w_{\text{pred}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} + w_{\text{dec}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}} + w_{\text{var}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}\;}
$$

with $w_{\text{pred}} = 0.5$. All four components — encoder $E_\phi$, decoder $D_\psi$, aux propagator $P_{\text{aux}}$ — are trained jointly with a single AdamW optimizer.

Key points:

- **$P_{\text{aux}}$ is deliberately tiny.** Forcing the encoder to produce latents that even a 22k-parameter model can step forward is a much stronger constraint than allowing a large model. It pressures the encoder to learn a representation in which dynamics are simple, not just one in which a large network can pick up the slack.
- **Pushforward in physical space, not latent.** The targets $u_{n+k}$ are fixed ground truth. A latent-space target would be $z_{n+k}^\ast = E_\phi(u_{n+k})$, which moves as $\phi$ trains; this leads to a known degeneracy in which the encoder collapses to easy-to-predict (e.g. nearly constant) latents.
- **Window translation augmentation.** Each window of 4 snapshots is shifted by one common $\Delta x \in \{0,\ldots,N_x-1\}$, preserving the dynamics across the window.
- **Aux propagator is discarded** after v5 training. The stage-2 propagator is trained fresh on v5 latents.

## 4. Stage 2: the propagator

### 4.1 Architecture

The latent propagator is a residual MLP that takes two consecutive latent vectors and returns the next one:

$$
P_\theta:\;\mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d, \qquad
(z_{n-1}, z_n) \mapsto z_{n+1}.
$$

Internally:

1. **Concatenate and lift.** $x \leftarrow W_{\text{in}}\,[\,z_{n-1};\,z_n\,] + b_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^H$.
2. **Residual blocks.** For $\ell = 1, \ldots, L_b$:
   $$
   x \;\leftarrow\; x + W_2^{(\ell)}\,\mathrm{GELU}\bigl(W_1^{(\ell)}\,\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x) + b_1^{(\ell)}\bigr) + b_2^{(\ell)}.
   $$
3. **Output a delta and add to $z_n$.**
   $$
   \delta \;=\; W_{\text{out}}\,\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x) + b_{\text{out}},
   \qquad z_{n+1} \;=\; z_n + \delta.
   $$

Design choices and why:

- **Residual (delta) parameterization.** The model predicts the *change* in $z$, not the next $z$ directly. With $\delta = 0$ the model is the identity (persistence). This is the right prior for a small-stride dynamical system.
- **Zero-init the final layer:** $W_{\text{out}} \equiv 0$, $b_{\text{out}} \equiv 0$ at initialization. At step 0 the model is *exactly* the identity. A 16-step rollout at the first iteration produces a constant trajectory instead of exploding noise. The model only has to learn how to *move*; it never has to find persistence by accident.
- **Two-step input.** Using $(z_{n-1}, z_n)$ rather than $z_n$ alone gives the model access to a discrete velocity proxy. Useful for second-order behavior.

Final configuration after iteration: $L_b = 3$ blocks, $H = 128$, hidden FF width 128, dropout 0.1. Total parameters: ~109k.

### 4.2 Autoregressive rollout

To predict $K$ steps ahead, the propagator is applied recursively, advancing a 2-step sliding window:

$$
z_{n+k+1} = z_{n+k} + P_\theta(z_{n+k-1},\, z_{n+k}), \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, K-1.
$$

Optionally, during training only, per-step Gaussian noise can be injected:

$$
z_{n+k+1} \;\leftarrow\; z_{n+k+1} + \sigma_{\text{step}}\,\varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d).
$$

This is set to zero at evaluation time.

### 4.3 Training objective

The training data are windows of latent trajectories,

$$
\bigl(z_{n-1}, z_n\bigr) \to \bigl(z_{n+1}, z_{n+2}, \ldots, z_{n+K_{\max}}\bigr),
\qquad K_{\max} = 16.
$$

Let $\hat z_{n+k}$ be the autoregressive prediction. The base pushforward loss with horizon weights $w_k = \gamma^k / \sum_{j=1}^{K} \gamma^j$ is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{push}} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K_{\text{now}}} w_k\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{d}\|\hat z_{n+k} - z_{n+k}\|^2\right].
$$

An optional variance-match term, included in some experiments, penalizes amplitude collapse:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{varmatch}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl\|\mathrm{std}_k(\hat z) - \mathrm{std}_k(z)\bigr\|^2\right],
$$

where $\mathrm{std}_k$ is the standard deviation across the time axis of the rollout. Total:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{prop}} \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{push}} + w_{\text{varmatch}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{varmatch}}.
$$

### 4.4 Training schedules

Three time-varying schedules are used:

**Horizon curriculum.** $K_{\text{now}}$ starts at 2 and increases linearly to $K_{\max}$ over $E_{\text{warm}}$ epochs:

$$
K_{\text{now}}(e) = 2 + \left\lfloor \min(e / E_{\text{warm}},\, 1) \cdot (K_{\max} - 2) + 0.5\right\rfloor.
$$

The reason: pushforward on a chaotic system at full $K_{\max}$ from epoch 0 dominates the loss with far-future terms that are mostly noise. Starting small lets the model learn 1-step dynamics first.

**Input-pair noise.** $\sigma_{\text{in}}(e)$ anneals linearly between initial and final values. This regularizes against overfitting the exact training initial conditions.

**Per-step rollout noise.** $\sigma_{\text{step}}(e)$ anneals similarly. Trains the model to recover from its own errors.

In the latest configuration, $w_{\text{varmatch}} = 0$, $\gamma = 1$ (flat weights), and $\sigma_{\text{step}} = 0$, because these knobs were not contributing positively at the operating point we'd reached. They remain in the code as available levers.

### 4.5 Shift augmentation in latent space

To match the AE's translation equivariance and increase effective coverage, each physical training trajectory is encoded under multiple cyclic shifts before being treated as latent training data:

$$
z^{(j)}_n = E_\phi\bigl(\mathrm{Roll}(u_n, c_j)\bigr), \qquad c_j \in \{0,\, N_x/4,\, N_x/2,\, 3N_x/4\}.
$$

For 50 train trajectories and 4 shifts each, we obtain 200 latent trajectories of length $T$.

### 4.6 Best-val checkpointing

The propagator overfits within a few epochs (val multi-step error starts climbing while train continues to fall). To avoid throwing away the good model, the checkpoint is saved whenever validation $K_{\max}$-step error improves, and the final evaluation reloads that best checkpoint.

## 5. The physical-space diagnostic

After multiple iterations of stage 2 failed to produce a propagator that beat persistence on rollout, we ran a diagnostic to determine whether the dynamics themselves were the obstacle or the latent representation was.

The setup: train a propagator of the same architecture and approximately matched parameter count (~150k vs latent's 110k) on the *physical* trajectories directly. State dimension becomes $N_x = 128$ instead of $d = 24$. Same data, same training recipe, same loss.

The result: the physical-space propagator beat persistence by roughly an order of magnitude over the entire 1–80 step lead window. Per-element RMSE at lead 20 was ~0.06 (physical prop) vs ~0.4 (persistence). The latent propagator at the same lead was on top of persistence.

Conclusion: the v4 latent representation is the bottleneck. KS dynamics at stride 5 are learnable; v4 latents discard the information needed to learn them. This motivated v5.

---

## 6. Why the v4 latent was a bad state variable

The v4 loss optimizes for a single property of the latent: that $D_\psi(E_\phi(u)) \approx u$. Nothing in the v4 objective references the *dynamics* of the KS flow.

In particular, if two physical states $u^{(a)}$ and $u^{(b)}$ are far apart on the attractor but the encoder happens to map them to nearby latent vectors (because their reconstructions both score well), they will have *different* futures despite having nearly identical $(z_{n-1}, z_n)$ histories. The conditional distribution of $z_{n+1}$ given $(z_{n-1}, z_n)$ is broad. The MSE-optimal next-step prediction in latent space is then close to the *conditional mean*, which on a chaotic attractor is close to $z_n$ itself — i.e. **persistence**.

The v5 modification puts the dynamics directly in the loss: the encoder is rewarded when the *decoded predictions* match physical truth. This breaks the degeneracy. Distinct physical futures must arise from distinct latent histories, so the encoder is forced to spread them.

## 8. What runs the pipeline end-to-end

A complete forecast for $K$ steps starting from a physical-state pair $(u_{n-1}, u_n)$:

1. **Encode.** $z_{n-1} = E_\phi(u_{n-1}),\quad z_n = E_\phi(u_n).$
2. **Roll.** For $k = 0, \ldots, K-1$:
   $$ z_{n+k+1} = z_{n+k} + P_\theta(z_{n+k-1},\, z_{n+k}). $$
3. **Decode (where needed).** $\hat u_{n+k} = D_\psi(z_{n+k}).$

For nonlinear data assimilation we will plug this forecast model into a cycling scheme. The plan is:

1. **Ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) in latent space.** Maintain $N$ ensemble members $\{z_n^{(i)}\}$. Propagate each independently with $P_\theta$. At observation times, decode to physical space, apply a (typically linear) observation operator $H$, and perform the standard EnKF analysis update using the ensemble sample covariance.
2. **Diagnostics.** Decoded analysis vs truth; RMSE vs time; ensemble spread vs error (consistency); comparison to free-running forecast.
3. **Upgrades if needed.** Particle filter for strong nonlinearity, 4D-Var-in-latent using autodiff through $P_\theta$.
