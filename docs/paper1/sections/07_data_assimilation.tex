%======================================================================
\section{Data assimilation in the local latent field}
\label{sec:da_results}
%======================================================================

Part~II asks whether the local structure that lets a model transfer also
lets a filter work, with one model and one setting, on domains of any
size, and what such a filter is worth against one built on the
equations. The filter runs entirely in the latent space of a learned
model and observes the physical field. It never uses the equations of
the system, and in 2D its model never sees a clean field.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Setting}
\label{sec:da_setting}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\para{The model.} The 2D experiments use one model throughout: the
architecture and window training of Part~I with $c=12$ channels per site
instead of 6, trained only on fields that carry one fixed realization of
independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation $0.1$ in standardized
units, the level of the observation errors below. It never sees a clean
field. Its Kaplan--Yorke dimension is within $3.6\%$ of the truth's at
$44^2$ and $2.1\%$ at $66^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}), comparable to the
6-channel model of Part~I. We use the larger model for data assimilation
because the filter benefits from the extra capacity, and keep the
6-channel model for Part~I, whose transfer results it established. The
1D experiments use the 1D model of Part~I.

\para{Twin experiments.} At each domain size the truth is a trajectory of
the PDE, integrated at the deployment resolution from a spun-up state.
Every $0.5$ time units (5 model steps, about $0.08$ Lyapunov times) the
field is observed at the points of a regular network with spacing
$2.75$, half the patch length, so four observations per site, with
independent Gaussian errors of standard deviation $0.1$ in standardized
units ($0.62$ in field units). The networks are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:da_networks}. Each tiling's initial ensemble is its
encoding of the initial truth plus independent perturbations of
standard deviation $0.1$ per latent coordinate. Each run has $100$
cycles, and all reported values are steady-state averages over cycles
$51$--$100$.

\para{The filter.} The per-site local ensemble transform filter of
Sec.~\ref{sec:da_method}, Gaspari--Cohn half-width $1.5$ sites, RTPS
$\alpha=0.7$, model noise $0.02$ per coordinate and step, $64$ members per
tiling and four shifted tilings blended by Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend}. These
values are the same in every experiment of this section; none was tuned
per domain size, per seed or per experiment.

\para{What is measured.} The \emph{error} is the root-mean-square
difference between the analysis mean, decoded and blended, and the true
field, over all grid points, in field units. We compare it with what can
be had without a dynamical model: cubic-spline interpolation of the same
observations ($0.81$--$0.83$ at every size) and climatological optimal
interpolation ($0.80$ at $44^2$); with the decoder's own error on the
encoded truth, $\|\Dec(\E(u))-u\|$, single and blended (the \emph{decoder
floor}); and with a filter that knows the equations, the physical-space
local ensemble transform filter of Sec.~\ref{sec:da_method} run with the
true PDE ($0.076$ at its best localization). The run without assimilation
has errors of $4.2$--$5.3$. \emph{Calibration} is the ratio of ensemble
spread to error, $1$ for an ensemble that represents its own
uncertainty. Free-run skill, the ratio of the free-run error to the
analysis error, is reported in Table~\ref{tab:da_2d_sizes} for comparison
with earlier latent filters.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Shifted tilings}
\label{sec:da_tilings}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

With one tiling the analysis error at $44^2$ is $0.457$ (seed 0; $0.431$ and
$0.418$ for seeds 1 and 2). With four shifted tilings it is $0.324$, $0.310$
and $0.302$: about $30\%$ lower on every seed, from the same model with no
retraining (Fig.~\ref{fig:da_2d}f and Table~\ref{tab:da_2d_ensemble}). Two
tilings, offset diagonally, give $0.369$, and sixteen, offset by a quarter
patch, $0.287$. The calibration is $0.86$ with one tiling, $0.92$--$0.99$ with
four and $0.51$ with sixteen: the independent spreads average down faster
than the errors once the tilings overlap strongly, so four is the
calibrated choice and sixteen the accurate one. Uniform weights instead
of Hann weights give $0.313$ at calibration $0.63$.

\para{It is the offsets, not the members.} Four ensembles of 64 members on
the \emph{same} tiling, blended the same way, give $0.450$, $0.423$ and $0.412$,
no better than one tiling, and a calibration of $0.44$--$0.48$
(Table~\ref{tab:da_2d_ensemble}(iii)). Splitting a fixed total of 64
members into four tilings of 16 gives $0.352$, $0.334$ and $0.318$, against
$0.457$, $0.431$ and $0.418$ for one ensemble of 64: at equal cost the tilings
are $23$--$24\%$ more accurate on every seed. Shifted tilings are a better use
of members than a larger ensemble.

\para{Why it works.} With one tiling the error is tied to the patch
lattice. It is largest at patch edges, $0.49$--$0.58$ against $0.38$ at patch
centres (Fig.~\ref{fig:tilings}c), and the mean gradient across a patch
edge is $1.73$ times that inside a patch, against $1.02$ in the truth
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tilings}f). The lattice-tied part is not limited to
the seams. Decomposed by wavenumber, the errors of the four tilings are
only weakly correlated: the mean correlation between tilings is $0.37$--$0.42$
at wavenumbers below $1.6$ and $0.11$ above, at both $44^2$ and $220^2$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tilings}e). Averaging weakly correlated errors removes most
of them: the blend reduces the error variance by a factor of $1.4$--$1.9$ at
wavenumbers below $1.6$ and $3.9$ above (Fig.~\ref{fig:tilings}d). The seam
ratio of the blend is $1.02$, the truth's. Feeding the blended analysis
back into each tiling's encoder makes things worse, because the blended
field is not the decoding of any latent state; the tilings therefore
cycle independently and are combined only for output.

\para{Exactness and cost.} Each tiling's filter is exactly local
(Proposition~\ref{prop:blockdiag}), and the blend is a fixed linear map
of their outputs, so the composite analysis is exactly local too. The
four filters are independent and run in parallel; run in one process,
four tilings cost about $2.3$ times one at $220^2$ in our earlier
measurement, because the truth integration and the observation draws are shared
\placeholder{P2: clean timing from I14} (Sec.~\ref{sec:da_cost}).

\subsection{Accuracy and calibration at the native size}
\label{sec:da_accuracy}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

At $44^2$ the analysis error is $0.30$--$0.32$ over three seeds, against $0.81$--$0.83$
for interpolating the same observations and $0.80$ for climatological
optimal interpolation: the learned dynamics carry information between
observation times and between observation points that the observations
alone do not. At the observed points the error is $0.31$--$0.33$, half the
observation error, so the analysis is not simply drawing towards the
data. The prior, one cycle earlier, has an error of $0.34$--$0.39$; the
latent model's own error over one cycle, measured on an independent
truth, is small, so the filter's error is a persistent part that the
analysis does not remove rather than error the model regrows each cycle.
The ensemble is well calibrated, with spread over error $0.92$--$0.99$.
RTPS weights of $0.5$, $0.7$ and $0.9$ give $0.322$, $0.324$ and $0.340$ at
calibrations $0.78$, $0.92$ and $1.16$; we use $0.7$, the calibrated choice
within $1\%$ of the best error.

\para{The decoder is not the limit.} The decoder floor is $0.24$--$0.25$ for one
tiling, about half of that tiling's analysis error, and $0.13$--$0.14$ for the
blend, well below the blended analysis's $0.30$--$0.33$
(Table~\ref{tab:da_2d_sizes}). Most of the remaining error is therefore
the filter's, not the reconstruction's.

\para{The analysis belongs in latent space.} One could use the learned
model only as a forecast device and do the analysis in physical space.
We tested this with the same model, encoding each member on the four
tilings, propagating, decoding and blending, and analysing the blended
members with the physical-space local ensemble transform filter of
Sec.~\ref{sec:da_method}, tuned over its localization radius and RTPS.
Its best error is $0.354$, $0.345$ and $0.338$ for seeds 0--2, at a calibration of
$1.22$--$1.27$, against $0.324$, $0.310$ and $0.302$ at $0.92$--$0.99$ for the latent
analysis; with one tiling the two are equal ($0.461$ and $0.457$). The latent
analysis is $5$--$10\%$ more accurate on every seed and better calibrated.
A physical-space analysis must re-encode fields that the decoder never
produced, and the latent analysis does not.

\para{Gaussianity.} The latent coordinates are close to Gaussian in
climatology (mean absolute skewness per coordinate $0.20$, excess kurtosis
$-0.07$), and 16-member one-cycle priors show mean absolute skewness of $0.38$ in
latent space and $0.40$ in physical space, within the sampling noise of about $0.6$
for 16 members. At this cycle length the latent space offers neither an
advantage nor a disadvantage for a Gaussian filter.

\subsection{One model and one setting from \texorpdfstring{$44^2$ to $440^2$}{44x44 to 440x440}}
\label{sec:da_2d}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:da_2d_sizes} and Fig.~\ref{fig:da_2d} give the result
that the locality was built for. The same model and the same filter
settings are deployed on square domains from $44^2$ to $440^2$, a factor of
100 in area and in latent dimension ($\dlat = 768$ to $76\,800$ per tiling).
The analysis error is $0.324$, $0.304$, $0.312$, $0.326$ and $0.311$ at $44^2$, $88^2$,
$220^2$, $330^2$ and $440^2$, with second seeds at $220^2$ and $330^2$ within $0.01$, and
the calibration stays between $0.92$ and $0.98$. With one tiling the error
is $0.42$--$0.46$ at every size; interpolation stays at $0.81$--$0.83$ and the
blended decoder floor at $0.13$--$0.14$. Nothing degrades with the domain,
and there is no sign of a size beyond which the method stops working
(Fig.~\ref{fig:da_2d}b,d).

\para{The ensemble does not grow with the domain.} With four tilings, the
error is $0.450$, $0.352$, $0.330$, $0.324$ and $0.322$ for $8$, $16$, $32$, $64$ and $128$
members per tiling at $44^2$, and $0.329$, $0.315$, $0.312$ and $0.311$ for $16$ to $128$ at
$220^2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:da_2d}c): accuracy saturates by about 32 members per
tiling at both sizes. Equation~\eqref{eq:nens_local} with the 2D KS
density of $0.043$ per unit area and the taper's support (radius $16.5$
length units) gives about $37$, consistent with that saturation.

\subsection{Learned versus physics-based filters under model error}
\label{sec:da_model_error}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

No forecast model of a real system is correct, and for many systems of
interest, convection-permitting weather models among them, the model
error is large, structural and poorly known. Measurement error, by
contrast, is usually well characterized, which is why the model of this
section is trained on noisy fields. The question is then not whether a
learned filter beats a filter that knows the equations; with the true
equations the physics-based filter reaches $0.076$, four times better than
the latent filter's $0.31$. It is how wrong a physical model has to be
before a model learned from noisy data is the better choice, once the
physics-based filter is given every standard remedy for model error.

\para{Design.} The truth is the 2D KS equation, the system the latent
model was trained on, and its error, $0.312$ averaged over seeds 0--2
($0.302$--$0.324$), is fixed: it was not tuned for this section. The
comparator is the physical-space local ensemble transform filter of
Sec.~\ref{sec:da_method}, with the same observations, whose forecast
model is wrong in one of four ways (Appendix~\ref{app:da}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{a wrong coefficient}, $a\nabla^2u$ with $a=1.1$ or $1.25$;
\item \emph{a spurious process}: uniform advection at speed $1$ or $2$, or a
  fixed terrain-like forcing of amplitude $0.2$ or $0.4$;
\item \emph{a coarse grid}, $64^2$ or $32^2$ instead of $128^2$;
\item \emph{a wrong form of the damping}: $-a\nabla^2u - b(-\nabla^2)^{p/2}u -
  (g/2)|\nabla u|^2$ with damping order $p=5$, $6$ or $8$ instead of $4$, whose three
  coefficients are all estimated online, so that no coefficient is left
  wrong but no setting of them reproduces the KS growth-rate curve.
\end{itemize}
Each physics-based filter is given, and tuned on seed 0 over, the RTPS
weight and the localization radius; online estimation of the parameter
it gets wrong (the coefficient, the advection speed, or $a$, $b$ and $g$
jointly); and bias correction with additive model error estimated
offline from one-cycle residuals on an independent trajectory. Its
reported value is its best configuration on seed 0, confirmed on seeds
1--2; the decision rule (a filter wins if its three-seed mean is at least
$10\%$ lower and it is lower on every seed) was written down before the
runs.

\para{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:da_model_error} and Fig.~\ref{fig:model_error} order the cases by the
physics-based filter's difficulty. It wins clearly when its model error
is one it can correct: with the $\nabla^2$ coefficient $10\%$ off and estimated
online it reaches $0.225$; bias correction removes the fixed forcing
entirely ($0.225$ at both amplitudes); and the coarse grids lose little
($0.083$ and $0.181$; bias correction does not help there). It loses when the error is larger or of the wrong form:
with the coefficient $25\%$ off, online estimation brings it from $0.48$ to
$0.42$, still $1.35$ times the latent filter's error; with spurious
advection, estimation of the speed leaves $0.44$ and $0.55$, $1.4$ and $1.8$
times. With the wrong damping order, where no coefficient is left wrong, the tuned
filter reaches $0.283$, $0.343$ and $0.409$ for $p = 5$, $6$ and $8$. Joint online
estimation of $a$, $b$ and $g$ brings orders 5 and 6 to $0.263$ and $0.322$ and
does not help order 8: the physics-based filter wins at $p=5$ ($1.19$ times),
ties at $p=6$, and loses at $p=8$ ($1.31$ times); bias
correction does nothing for these state-dependent errors ($0.297$, $0.361$
and $0.440$ on seed 0).

\para{The crossover.} What decides the comparison is not the size of the
physics model's one-cycle forecast error but its \emph{state-dependent}
part: the residual left after subtracting the mean one-cycle error,
which bias correction removes. The fixed forcing of amplitude $0.4$ has a
one-cycle error of $0.20$, but only $0.016$ of it is state-dependent, and the
physics-based filter wins. Every case whose state-dependent one-cycle
error is below $0.08$ (the grids, the forcing, the $10\%$ coefficient, damping
order 5, $0.000$--$0.077$) is a win or a tie for the physics-based filter; every
case at $0.2$ or above (the $25\%$ coefficient, the advection, damping order
8) is a win for the latent filter; and order 6, at $0.12$, is a tie. The crossover is therefore near $0.1$ in field units, a
sixth of the observation error: a physical model whose one-cycle error,
after bias correction, exceeds about a sixth of the observation error is
worth replacing by a model learned from data, and one below it is not.
The hardest case for both filters is a truth with a process that neither can represent: an
unobserved field $v$ with its own slow dynamics, $u_t=\mathrm{KS}(u)+\varepsilon v$,
$v_t=-v/\tau+\kappa\nabla^2u$, $\tau=2$, whose memory (4--4.5 time units, eight to nine cycles) makes $v$ a
function of the past of $u$, not of its present: about $20\%$ of its variance is not predictable from
the current field. The latent model is trained on noisy fields $u$ of this coupled system, so the
slow process acts in all its training data, but it never sees $v$; the physics-based filter's model
is KS without $v$, given every remedy (Appendix~\ref{app:da_comparator}). At $\varepsilon=0.1$, where the
state-dependent one-cycle error of the KS model is $0.15$, about $0.18$ of this system's observation
error, the latent filter's error is $0.476$ ($0.465$--$0.483$) and the physics-based filter's best, with
the $\nabla^2$ coefficient estimated online, $0.442$ ($0.436$--$0.447$): a tie by the decision rule, as the
crossover predicts. Neither is far from what the true equations achieve when $v$ is unobserved
($0.405$, the same filter carrying its own $v$), and both are well below interpolation ($1.10$--$1.15$).
\placeholder{P7: $\varepsilon=0.2$ (state-dependent one-cycle error $0.35$, about $0.4$ of the observation
error), where the crossover predicts a clear win for the latent filter.}

\para{Robustness.} The physics-based filter works only in a narrow window
of RTPS. With damping order 6 its error is $0.72$, $0.43$, $0.35$, $0.93$ and $1.27$ at
RTPS $0.5$, $0.6$, $0.7$, $0.9$ and $1.0$ ($c=16$), and multiplicative inflation
without RTPS diverged or was far worse in every wrong-model case. At its
best setting it is over-dispersed, with spread over error $1.7$--$2.0$. The
latent filter used one setting throughout and was not retuned for any
case. \placeholder{P9: the same comparison at $220^2$, if J5 is run.}

\para{What this does and does not show.} The comparison is between a
learned model with complete noisy training fields of the true system and
a physical model with a chosen, not calibrated, error; real training data
are sparser, and real model errors are not of our choosing. The latent
filter's error is about four times that of a filter with the correct
equations, so where a trustworthy model exists it should be used. The
result is the location of the crossover and the observation that it is
set by the state-dependent part of the model error, which is the part
that parameter estimation and bias correction cannot reach.

\subsection{Trained only on noisy data}
\label{sec:da_noisy}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The model of this part was trained on fields that carry observation-level
noise, and it is the better for it. A model with the same architecture and
recipe trained on clean fields gives $0.338$, $0.321$, $0.326$, $0.340$ and $0.326$ at
$44^2$ to $440^2$ (four tilings, seed 0), against $0.324$, $0.304$, $0.312$, $0.326$ and $0.311$
for the noisy-trained model: $4$--$5\%$ lower error at every size, with the
calibration rising from $0.85$--$0.90$ to $0.92$--$0.98$. At $44^2$ the gain holds on all
three seeds ($0.338$, $0.322$, $0.320$ against $0.324$, $0.310$, $0.302$). The dynamics are
preserved: the noisy-trained model's Kaplan--Yorke dimension is within
$3.6\%$ and $2.1\%$ of the truth's at $44^2$ and $66^2$, against $2.1\%$ and $1.1\%$ for the
clean-trained one, both within the truth's own spread.

\para{Why.} The decoder floor falls from $0.27$--$0.29$ to $0.24$--$0.25$ with one tiling and
from $0.15$--$0.16$ to $0.13$--$0.14$ with four, and the seam ratio of a single
tiling from $1.94$ to $1.73$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:tilings}f). Passing perturbed true
states through the encoder and decoder shows where this comes from
(Appendix~\ref{app:da}): the noisy-trained autoencoder reconstructs clean
states $15\%$ more accurately ($0.039$ against $0.046$ in standardized units) and
removes $15$--$20\%$ more grid-scale noise (it leaves $44\%$ of white noise of
standard deviation $0.1$, against $53\%$), while neither model pulls smooth or
latent-space perturbations back towards the attractor; those pass through
unchanged. The benefit is better reconstruction and small-scale
denoising, not a projection of the analysis onto the attractor.

\subsection{Forecasts, robustness and observation density}
\label{sec:da_robust}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\para{Forecasts from the analyses.} A better analysis should give a better
forecast, not only a smoother picture. From steady-state analyses at
$44^2$ we forecast each tiling's analysis mean with the learned model,
without noise, and blend the forecasts (Fig.~\ref{fig:forecasts},
Table~\ref{tab:da_forecast}). The blended forecast's error is $26\%$ below
that of the single-tiling forecast after one cycle and $15$--$17\%$ below it
at $0.4$--$1.7$ Lyapunov times. It reaches half the field's standard
deviation after $1.26$ Lyapunov times (seeds 0--2: $1.16$--$1.40$), against $1.10$ for
the single-tiling analysis, $1.62$ for a forecast started from the encoded
true state and $0.33$ for persistence, and the field's standard deviation
after $2.39$, against $2.06$, $2.70$ and $0.74$. The tilings recover a third to a half
of the forecast skill that separates the single-tiling analysis from a
perfect initial condition for this model. Beyond about three Lyapunov
times all forecasts are decorrelated from the truth; the blend's error
then stays lower only because it averages four partly independent
forecasts.

\para{Robustness.} Started from an ensemble drawn from an independent
climatology, with no knowledge of the truth, the filter's error falls
from $1.33$ to within $10\%$ of its steady value in 8 cycles, and the analysis
then converges onto the one started from the encoded truth: the
difference between the two decays by a factor of about ten every ten
cycles, to $10^{-8}$ by cycle 100 (Table~\ref{tab:da_robust}). With the
observation error misspecified by a factor of two in either direction
the error rises by $4$--$8\%$ ($0.351$ and $0.338$ against $0.324$), and the calibration
moves the way it should ($0.58$ and $1.40$). A 300-cycle run at $220^2$ holds $0.31$ throughout.

\para{Observation density.} Table~\ref{tab:da_robust} and Fig.~\ref{fig:da_2d}
use observations every $2.75$ length units, four per site. With twice as
many in each direction (spacing $1.4$) the error is $0.226$ with four tilings and
$0.333$ with one, against $0.539$ for interpolation; with half as many (spacing
$5.5$, one observation per site) it is $0.769$ and $0.990$, against $3.01$ for
interpolation, a factor of four. At this sparse spacing the filter stays
bounded over 300 cycles (means $0.77$, $0.77$ and $0.79$ over cycles 51--100,
101--200 and 201--300), but its error relative to interpolation is no
longer the whole story: the learned model must carry the state between
sparse observations, and on longer runs with sparser networks the
stability of the learned propagator becomes the limit. Failsafes for that
regime, a propagator trained with latent noise, a gate that falls back to
it off the attractor, and the reset of a tiling that departs from the
others, are the subject of a follow-up study.

\subsection{The one-dimensional results}
\label{sec:da_1d}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Two results from 1D KS complete the picture (Fig.~\ref{fig:da_1d};
details, the table and the observation-density sweep in
Appendix~\ref{app:da_1d}). They use the 1D model of Part~I, deployed at
$L=200$ and $400$ without retraining, a single tiling, and the particle flow
filter or the stochastic ensemble Kalman filter at a fixed observation
spacing of $3.125$; skill is the ratio of free-run to analysis error.

\para{The ensemble an unlocalized filter needs doubles with the domain.}
With localization, every ensemble of 16 or more members gives a skill of
$2.3$--$3.0$ at both sizes and with both filters. Without it, the filter is
worse than no assimilation until the ensemble is large enough, and how
large depends on the domain: at $L=200$, where the truth has $n_+=26$--$27$, it is
marginal with 64 members and works with 128; at $L=400$, where $n_+=50$--$51$, it
fails with 64, is marginal with 128 and works only with 256. The two
filters differ by at most $0.4$ in skill. This is Eq.~\eqref{eq:nens_local}
and its unlocalized counterpart, measured.

\para{A distance beats a distance-free correction.} Sampling error
correction \citep{anderson2012} localizes without a distance and is the
natural alternative for a latent space that has none. On one latent at
$L=200$, in the forms in which it runs (the corrected matrix projected onto
the positive semidefinite cone, or the serial correction of each
regression coefficient that \citet{anderson2012} uses), Gaspari--Cohn
localization in the latent field beats it at every ensemble size below
256, by a factor of up to $1.7$ against the projected form and up to $3.5$
against the serial one, which does not help at all below 128 members.
SEC removes sampling noise from correlations; a distance taper also
removes the many weak true correlations between distant sites, which a
small ensemble cannot afford to estimate. Giving the latent a geometry is
what makes this possible.

\subsection{Cost and memory}
\label{sec:da_cost}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:da_cost} gives what the filters cost. Memory is the peak
resident memory of the filter alone, measured as the peak over a run
minus the peak once the truth has been generated. The per-site filter
needs $0.12$, $0.39$, $0.56$, $3.35$ and $4.30$~GB at $\dlat=384$, $864$, $1536$,
$9600$ and $21\,600$. From $88^2$ to $220^2$ the latent dimension grows
$6.25$-fold and the memory $5.96$-fold, and from $220^2$ to $330^2$ the
dimension grows $2.25$-fold and the memory $1.28$-fold: the filter's memory
grows no faster than the domain area, as Proposition~\ref{prop:blockdiag}
implies. The dense filter needs the
same at the native size but $16.75$~GB at $220^2$, about 24 copies of one
$\dlat\times\dlat$ matrix; at $330^2$ a single such matrix is $3.5$~GB and at
$440^2$ it is $11$~GB, so we did not run it there.

Time is less favourable to the per-site filter at small sizes. Over a
whole run, including the integration of the PDE truth, the per-site
filter takes two to four times the dense filter's CPU time up to $88^2$,
because it loops over sites in Python while the dense filter performs a
few large matrix products. The two cross between $88^2$ and $220^2$
($1450$ against $1914$~s at $220^2$), and from there the per-site filter's
time grows in proportion to the area ($\times4.4$ from $220^2$ to $440^2$
for $\times4$ in area). The deterministic variant is about $40\%$ faster
than the perturbed-observation one, having no observation draws. The
CPU times carry run-to-run noise of tens of percent from other jobs on
the same machine (the $66^2$ point is slower than the $88^2$ one); we
have not profiled the per-site loop, which is the obvious target for a
vectorized implementation. The observation Jacobian itself is cheap:
its twelve forward passes take $2.4$~ms at $44^2$ and $119$~ms at $440^2$, where
dense reverse mode would need one pass per observation
(Appendix~\ref{app:da_jacobian}).
